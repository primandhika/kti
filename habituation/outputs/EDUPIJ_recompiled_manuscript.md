**[1. Introduction]{.mark}**

Speaking is a foundational component of language development in primary
education, functioning not only as a means of communication but also as
a medium through which children construct meaning, organize ideas, and
demonstrate understanding. At the elementary level, speaking activities
ideally support the development of oral fluency, coherence, and
communicative confidence while simultaneously fostering higher-order
cognitive engagement. However, research across diverse educational
contexts indicates that speaking instruction in primary classrooms often
remains limited to surface-level performance, emphasizing correctness
and fluency rather than reflective language use (Goh & Burns, 2012).

In the Indonesian primary education context, speaking instruction tends
to be strongly teacher-directed and product-oriented. Classroom speaking
activities frequently take the form of answering factual questions,
retelling texts, or reproducing model sentences provided by teachers or
textbooks. While such practices may support basic oral production, they
rarely encourage learners to reflect on what they intend to say, how
they express their ideas, or how effectively their message is conveyed
to others. As a result, speaking is commonly treated as an output
activity rather than a cognitive and reflective process. This condition
mirrors broader findings in language education research showing that
young learners' oral language development is often constrained by
instructional routines that prioritize reproduction and memorization
over meaning-making and self-regulation (Goh & Burns, 2012; Mercer &
Dawes, 2014).

The limited integration of metacognitive processes further compounds
this issue. Metacognitive awareness refers to learners' consciousness of
their own thinking processes and their ability to plan, monitor, and
evaluate learning activities (Flavell, 1979). In speaking, metacognitive
awareness enables learners to anticipate content before speaking,
monitor clarity and coherence during speech, and reflect on performance
afterward. These processes are particularly important for young
learners, as early exposure to metacognitive practices contributes to
the gradual development of self-regulated learning behaviours
(Whitebread et al., 2009). Despite this, metacognitive instruction in
primary education has predominantly focused on reading and writing,
while speaking remains underexplored as a domain for metacognitive
development, especially in the Indonesian context.

Existing scholarship has repeatedly shown that speaking instruction in
primary classrooms remains predominantly reproductive and procedural in
nature. Mercer and Dawes (2014) and Goh and Burns (2012) note that
classroom talk is often structured to elicit brief, teacher-expected
responses, positioning students as language reproducers rather than
meaning-makers.
In such contexts, learners are seldom encouraged to engage in reflective
processes before speaking, to monitor the coherence and clarity of their
utterances while speaking, or to evaluate the effectiveness of their
communication afterward (Mercer & Dawes, 2014; Whitebread et al.,
2009). Studies examining metacognition in oral language learning
further suggest that feedback practices in speaking lessons are largely
external and evaluative, emphasizing accuracy and fluency over
reflective self-regulation (Whitebread et al., 2009; Andrade &
Brookhart, 2020).
Therefore, opportunities for learners to internalize evaluative criteria
and develop awareness of their own speaking processes remain limited.
When reflective support is absent, speaking activities can become
disconnected from conscious cognitive regulation, reducing students'
capacity to plan, monitor, and adapt their oral language use across
contexts.

Taken together, these findings reveal a persistent mismatch between
contemporary calls for meaningful, reflective learning and the realities
of speaking pedagogy in primary education. While theoretical and policy
discourses increasingly emphasize learner awareness, self-regulation,
and deep learning (Kementerian Pendidikan Dasar dan Menengah Republik
Indonesia, 2025), everyday classroom practices continue to privilege
observable performance over the invisible metacognitive processes that
underpin effective and sustainable speaking development.

To address this limitation, the present study is grounded in the concept
of habituation as a pedagogical process. Habituation refers to the
formation of stable cognitive and behavioral patterns through repeated,
consistent practice in meaningful contexts (Lally et al., 2010). From a
developmental perspective, habituation is especially relevant for
primary school learners, as young children are more likely to
internalize cognitive routines through simple, repetitive activities
rather than abstract instruction. In this study, habituation is
conceptualized as the gradual embedding of metacognitive awareness into
students' speaking practices through recurring reflective routines.

Within this framework, metacognitive awareness in speaking is not taught
as an explicit concept but cultivated as a habitual practice embedded in
routine classroom activities. This approach aligns with calls for
pedagogical designs that support deep and meaningful learning while
remaining developmentally appropriate for primary students (Kementerian
Pendidikan Dasar dan Menengah Republik Indonesia, 2025).

Digital microlearning is positioned in this study as a supportive
learning context rather than a technological focus. Microlearning,
characterized by short, focused learning units, aligns well with the
attention span and learning preferences of primary students and has
gained popularity in elementary classrooms as a flexible digital medium
(Leong et al., 2021). When used strategically, digital microlearning can
scaffold brief reflective moments before, during, and after speaking
activities, thereby supporting the habituation of metacognitive
awareness without overburdening instructional time or teacher workload.

Despite growing interest in meaningful learning and digital integration
in primary education, a clear gap remains between these aspirations and
actual classroom practices in speaking instruction. Current approaches
rarely combine speaking, metacognitive habituation, and realistic
digital support in a coherent pedagogical design. This gap underscores
the need for empirical research that examines how metacognitive
awareness in speaking can be habituated through feasible classroom
interventions.

Accordingly, this study seeks to examine how metacognitive awareness
may begin to emerge in primary students' speaking through digital
microlearning-supported reflective routines. Rather than focusing on
short-term performance gains, the study adopts a process-oriented
perspective by investigating the formation of reflective speaking habits
over repeated learning cycles. Specifically, this study addresses the
following research questions: (1) what changes were observed in
students' planning, monitoring, and reflection behaviors during the
digital microlearning-supported speaking intervention; (2) what
observable behavioral indicators reflected the habituation of
metacognitive awareness across the speaking sessions; and (3) how did
students' written reflections indicate changes in their awareness of
speaking processes. By answering these questions, the study aims to
bridge the gap between the demand for meaningful, reflective learning
and the prevailing reproductive practices of speaking instruction in
primary education.

**2. Literature Review**

The literature informing this study can be organized around four related
areas: speaking pedagogy in primary education, metacognitive awareness
and self-regulated learning, habituation as routine formation, and
microlearning as a lightweight digital scaffold. Bringing these strands
together is necessary because the present study does not examine
speaking, metacognition, or educational technology in isolation;
rather, it investigates how reflective speaking routines can become a
stable classroom practice for young learners.

### **Speaking Pedagogy and the Limits of Performance-Oriented Instruction**

Research on speaking instruction has long noted that classroom practice
often privileges visible oral performance, such as fluency,
pronunciation, and accuracy, over the cognitive work that enables
learners to organize and regulate their speech (Goh & Burns, 2012).
Within primary classrooms, talk is frequently shaped by recitation,
brief responses, and teacher-controlled turn taking, which can limit
opportunities for students to use speaking as a tool for constructing
meaning (Mercer & Dawes, 2014). Such conditions are particularly
problematic in language learning because speaking is not merely the
production of correct utterances; it also involves selecting ideas,
monitoring intelligibility, and adjusting communication in real time.
When instruction remains product-oriented, young learners may complete
speaking tasks without developing awareness of how successful speaking
is planned, monitored, and improved.

### **Metacognitive Awareness in Language Learning**

Metacognition is commonly understood as knowledge of cognition and the
regulation of cognition, especially through planning, monitoring, and
evaluation (Flavell, 1979; Veenman et al., 2006). In educational
settings, these processes are central to self-regulated learning because
they enable learners to become more deliberate, strategic, and adaptive
in their academic behavior. Evidence from child development research
shows that even young learners can display emerging metacognitive and
self-regulatory behaviors when these are supported through structured,
observable routines rather than abstract explanation alone (Whitebread
et al., 2009). In classroom assessment research, reflective prompting
and feedback are also seen as mechanisms that help learners gradually
co-regulate and then internalize evaluative judgments about their own
work (Andrade & Brookhart, 2020). However, this line of work has been
applied far more often to reading, writing, and general learning tasks
than to oral language production. As a result, speaking remains a
comparatively underdeveloped site for metacognitive intervention,
especially in primary education where reflection is often assumed to be
too advanced or too time-consuming for everyday classroom use.

### **Habituation and the Internalization of Reflective Routines**

The concept of habituation helps explain how metacognitive behavior may
become sustainable in young learners' speaking practices. Habit
formation research suggests that stable routines emerge through repeated
actions performed in consistent contexts, with automaticity increasing
over time as learners become familiar with the cue-routine pattern
(Lally et al., 2010). Although habit research is not identical to
classroom learning theory, it offers a useful lens for understanding why
small, recurring reflective actions may be more effective than isolated
metacognitive lessons. For primary students, repeated prompts to think
before speaking, notice clarity while speaking, and reflect afterward
may gradually shift reflective behavior from teacher-dependent activity
to learner-initiated practice. This perspective is consistent with the
view that young children develop self-regulation most effectively when
cognitive expectations are embedded in recurring classroom routines that
are concrete, manageable, and socially supported (Whitebread et al.,
2009).

### **Digital Microlearning as a Supportive Classroom Scaffold**

Microlearning is generally characterized by short, focused learning
units that address a limited objective within a brief time span. Recent
reviews suggest that microlearning is most useful when it reduces
cognitive overload, supports just-in-time engagement, and fits easily
within broader learning sequences rather than replacing them altogether
(Leong et al., 2021). In school settings, this makes microlearning a
promising support for primary learners, whose attention and working
memory are better served by concise prompts than by lengthy
metacognitive explanations. Importantly, the value of microlearning in
this study lies not in technological novelty but in pedagogical
function: brief digital prompts can cue planning before speaking,
monitoring during performance, and reflection after speaking, while
remaining feasible within ordinary lesson time.

Taken together, the literature indicates three important points. First,
speaking instruction in primary education often lacks systematic support
for reflective regulation. Second, metacognitive processes can be
cultivated in young learners when they are operationalized through
concrete and repeated routines. Third, microlearning offers a practical
way to deliver those routines in short, developmentally appropriate
bursts. What remains insufficiently examined is how these elements can
be integrated into a coherent classroom design that treats
metacognitive awareness in speaking as a habit to be formed, not merely
a skill to be explained. This unresolved gap provides the rationale for
the present study.

**3. Methodology**

This study employed a classroom-based exploratory intervention design
to investigate the emergence of metacognitive awareness in primary
students' speaking practices. The methodological orientation of the
study draws on habit formation theory, which conceptualizes learning as
the gradual internalization of cognitive routines through repeated and
consistent practice in authentic contexts (Lally et al., 2010). Rather
than testing causal effectiveness, the study was designed to document
how metacognitive speaking behaviors became increasingly observable and
more stable across repeated reflective routines.

Accordingly, the study used a single intact classroom with pre- and
post-intervention observation points and embedded qualitative analysis.
This design was selected because the study did not aim to compare
intervention effects across groups, but to examine how planning,
monitoring, and reflection behaviors emerged within naturally occurring
classroom practice. Such an approach is particularly appropriate for
primary education settings, where ethical, practical, and curricular
constraints often limit the feasibility of controlled experimental
designs.

The participants were 28 Grade 5 students enrolled at SDN Mandiri
Baros 1-6, Cimahi, Indonesia, aged between 10 and 11 years. This age
group was selected based on developmental research indicating that
children at this stage can display emerging metacognitive awareness
when supported through structured and concrete learning routines
(Whitebread et al., 2009). One intact Grade 5 classroom was selected at
the classroom level from the available school context because the
intervention was implemented through naturally occurring classroom
routines rather than through individual student assignment. All
students participated as part of their regular classroom learning
activities, and no exclusion criteria were applied, as the study aimed
to examine classroom-level patterns rather than individual differences.

The intervention was implemented over a period of four weeks, comprising
eight speaking sessions conducted twice per week. This duration was
determined with reference to habit formation research, which suggests
that observable behavioural regularities can begin to emerge through
repeated practice within a relatively short time frame, provided that
instructional routines are consistent and stable (Lally et al., 2010).
Each session followed an identical pedagogical structure to support
habituation rather than novelty-driven engagement.

Digital microlearning was used as a pedagogical scaffold to support
reflective speaking routines. The microlearning units consisted of
short, focused digital prompts accessed by students immediately before,
during, and after speaking activities. Each prompt was designed to take
approximately one to three minutes and to focus on one metacognitive
action at a time, such as identifying the first idea to say, checking
whether a message was clear, or naming one improvement for the next
task. These prompts were designed to elicit metacognitive awareness
related to planning, monitoring, and reflection, using language
appropriate for primary learners. Importantly, metacognitive concepts
were not introduced explicitly; instead, students encountered repeated
reflective cues across sessions, allowing awareness to develop through
repeated engagement. This design choice aligns with research suggesting
that young learners internalize metacognitive routines more effectively
through practice than through abstract explanation (Veenman et al.,
2006).

Each speaking session was organized into three pedagogical phases.
Before speaking, students engaged with microlearning prompts that
encouraged them to plan what they would say and how they would organize
their ideas. During speaking, students performed short oral tasks, such
as explaining a familiar topic or sharing personal experiences, while
being reminded to attend to clarity and coherence without interrupting
their speech. After speaking, students completed brief guided
reflections supported by microlearning prompts, focusing on what aspects
of their speaking were effective and what could be improved. The
reflective structure remained constant across all sessions to reinforce
habituation through repetition and consistency.

Data were collected from multiple sources to capture both observable
behavioral changes and students' reflective engagement. Observable
speaking behaviors were documented using a researcher-developed
observation rubric focused on indicators of metacognitive awareness,
including evidence of planning, monitoring, and reflection. In
addition, short written reflections produced by students were collected
periodically to capture their awareness of speaking processes. Teacher
observation notes were also gathered to document classroom dynamics and
changes in students' engagement over time. The use of multiple data
sources enabled triangulation and strengthened the credibility of the
findings (Miles et al., 2014).

**Observation Rubric and Scoring Procedure**

The observation rubric was developed to capture three metacognitive
speaking indicators: planning before speaking, monitoring during
speaking, and reflection after speaking. Each indicator was rated on a
three-point scale: 1 = not observed or only externally prompted, 2 =
partially observed but still inconsistent or teacher-dependent, and 3 =
consistently observed with increasing student initiative. For planning,
a score of 1 referred to beginning to speak without visible
preparation, a score of 2 referred to limited preparation with
prompting, and a score of 3 referred to independent pausing or idea
organization before speaking. For monitoring, a score of 1 referred to
no visible self-checking, a score of 2 referred to limited correction
with support, and a score of 3 referred to independent self-correction
or rephrasing while speaking. For reflection, a score of 1 referred to
brief affective or purely evaluative comments, a score of 2 referred to
identifying one aspect of performance with guidance, and a score of 3
referred to independently identifying what worked and what to improve
next.

The rubric was reviewed for construct alignment and developmental
appropriateness before use in the classroom. Because the study relied on
a single primary observer, the scores should be interpreted as
descriptive classroom evidence rather than as independently verified
behavioral measurement. To reduce observer bias, rubric-based
observations were triangulated with teacher notes and students' written
reflections.

**Pre- and Post-Intervention Measurement**

Pre-intervention scores were obtained from an initial speaking task
conducted before the digital microlearning routine was introduced.
Post-intervention scores were obtained from a comparable speaking task
conducted after the eighth session. Both tasks followed similar
classroom conditions and were scored using the same observation rubric.

Quantitative data from the observation rubric were analyzed
descriptively to identify patterns and trends in students' metacognitive
speaking behaviors across sessions. Means, standard deviations, minima,
maxima, and mean differences were calculated for the three core
indicators (planning, monitoring, and reflection) at pre- and
post-intervention stages, and overall metacognitive awareness scores
were derived from the average of these indicators. Because the rubric
used a three-point ordinal scale and the study was exploratory in
nature, the analysis emphasized descriptive patterns rather than
inferential testing.

Session-level behavioral data were calculated based on student-session
observations. Early sessions referred to Sessions 1 and 2, producing 56
observation units from 28 students across two sessions. Final sessions
referred to Sessions 7 and 8, also producing 56 observation units.
Percentages therefore represent the proportion of student-session
observations in which each target behavior was observed, rather than the
proportion of individual students. Additional session-by-session
percentages were examined to trace the gradual emergence of planning,
monitoring, and reflection behaviors across the eight-session
intervention.

Qualitative data from student reflections and teacher notes were
analyzed thematically using an inductive coding approach informed by the
core metacognitive dimensions of planning, monitoring, and reflection.
Rather than treating short reflection excerpts as frequency data, the
qualitative analysis focused on representative patterns in the way
students described their speaking processes across the intervention.
When excerpts originally written in Indonesian were presented in
English, the translations were meaning-preserving rather than strictly
word-for-word. Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings was
used to provide a coherent account of how metacognitive awareness began
to emerge through repeated reflective routines.

**5. Discussion**

This study suggests that primary students' speaking practices may
provide a viable context for developing metacognitive regulation when
reflective routines are repeatedly cued through digital microlearning
prompts. Rather than treating speaking only as oral performance, the
findings position speaking as a site where planning, monitoring, and
reflection can begin to become visible in routine classroom activity.
This extends prior metacognitive research, which has largely focused on
reading and writing, by drawing attention to speaking as a practical
domain for early metacognitive development in primary education
(Flavell, 1979; Whitebread et al., 2009).

In addressing the first research question, the findings suggest that
repeated reflective routines were associated with increasingly
observable planning, monitoring, and reflection behaviors rather than
with a one-time shift in performance. The gradual emergence of these
behaviors aligns with habit formation theory, which emphasizes that
stable routines embedded in meaningful contexts support the
internalization of cognitive regulation (Lally et al., 2010). For young
learners, this process is particularly relevant because metacognitive
behaviors are more likely to develop through repeated practice than
through abstract explanation alone (Veenman et al., 2006). The speaking
activities in this study created recurring opportunities for learners to
enact reflective behaviors, making metacognitive awareness more visible
within ordinary classroom routines.

Regarding the second research question, the observed behavioral
indicators offer concrete descriptive evidence of metacognitive
engagement in primary students' speaking. Pausing to plan before
speaking, self-correction during speech, and reflective evaluation
after speaking correspond to the core metacognitive processes of
planning, monitoring, and evaluation identified in metacognitive theory
(Flavell, 1979). Notably, reflection showed the largest quantitative
increase. One plausible interpretation is that post-speaking reflection
may be more accessible for primary students than real-time monitoring,
because it allows them to think back on their speaking after the
performance pressure has passed. This pattern is consistent with the
view that structured reflection can serve as an entry point for the
gradual development of self-regulation (Andrade & Brookhart, 2020).

The third research question concerned how students' written reflections
indicated changing awareness of speaking processes. The qualitative data
suggest that students moved from brief affective comments toward more
process-oriented statements about what they intended to say, when they
noticed a problem, and what they wanted to improve next. Within that
process, digital microlearning appeared to function as a supportive
cueing mechanism by providing concise and repeated prompts without
substantially increasing instructional complexity. This supports prior
research suggesting that microlearning is most useful when it supports
routine formation and cognitive focus rather than serving as a primary
content-delivery mechanism (Leong et al., 2021). In this sense, the
pedagogical value of microlearning in the present study seems to lie
less in technological novelty and more in its capacity to stabilize a
planning-monitoring-reflection sequence within limited classroom time.

Taken together, these findings contribute to the broader discourse on
speaking pedagogy by challenging performance-oriented approaches that
prioritize fluency and accuracy over reflective engagement (Goh & Burns,
2012; Mercer & Dawes, 2014). The study's contribution is not to claim
that digital microlearning improved speaking in a causal sense, but to
document how a simple reflective sequence can make metacognitive
behaviors more observable in primary classroom talk. This perspective
reframes speaking not merely as oral output but as a pedagogical site
for cultivating early self-regulatory awareness through feasible,
repeated, and low-burden classroom routines.

At the same time, the findings should be interpreted cautiously. The
study involved a single intact class, a relatively short intervention
period, and descriptive analysis without a comparison group. Therefore,
the results are better understood as evidence of an emerging classroom
process than as proof of causal effectiveness or long-term transfer.
The reflective data were also limited to short student responses and
teacher observation notes, which capture visible development but not the
full depth of students' internal reasoning. Future studies could extend
the intervention over a longer period, include comparative conditions,
and examine whether these speaking-related metacognitive routines
transfer to other language tasks such as listening, reading, or
writing.

**6. Conclusion**

This study suggests that metacognitive awareness in primary students'
speaking practices can be supported through repeated reflective routines
embedded in digital microlearning prompts. Across the intervention,
observed mean scores increased from 1.42 to 2.68 for planning, from
1.36 to 2.51 for monitoring, and from 1.18 to 2.74 for reflection,
while the overall metacognitive awareness score increased from 1.32 to
2.64. Session-level behavioral observations also indicated that pausing
to plan, self-correction, and voluntary reflection became more frequent
in the final sessions than in the early sessions. Qualitative
reflections further suggested that students gradually moved from brief
affective comments toward more process-oriented statements about what
they intended to say, how they monitored their speech, and what they
wanted to improve.

These findings should be interpreted as descriptive evidence of an
emerging classroom process rather than as proof of intervention
effectiveness. The study involved a single intact class, a relatively
short intervention period, and no comparison group. Within those
limits, the study contributes to primary speaking pedagogy by showing
how simple and repeated reflective cues may help make metacognitive
speaking behaviors more visible in everyday classroom practice.

**7. Suggestions**

Several practical and research implications follow from these findings.
First, primary teachers may consider embedding brief planning,
monitoring, and reflection prompts into routine speaking tasks rather
than reserving reflection for formal assessment only. Second, digital
microlearning should be used as a scaffold for short, repeated
cognitive cues, not as a substitute for interaction-rich speaking
practice. Third, school leaders and curriculum developers may support
similar interventions by providing simple digital resources and teacher
guidance for reflective classroom routines. For future research, longer
intervention periods, larger and more diverse samples, comparison-group
designs, and stronger reliability procedures for observational scoring
are needed to examine the durability, transferability, and causal
strength of metacognitive habituation in speaking.
